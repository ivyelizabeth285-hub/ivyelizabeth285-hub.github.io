Seismic Isolation Engineering: Design, Systems, Bearings, and Structural Applications

anitaa October 1, 2026

Seismic isolation engineering is a specialized branch of structural earthquake design focused on controlling how ground motion is transmitted into a building or other engineered structure. Rather than relying only on the strength, stiffness, and ductility of the superstructure to resist seismic forces, an isolated structure incorporates an engineered interface between the foundation and the supported structure. This interface can introduce controlled flexibility, sliding, damping, or other response-modifying behavior that changes the structure's dynamic response during an earthquake.

Effective seismic isolation is not simply a matter of selecting a bearing from a catalog. It requires coordination among seismic hazard evaluation, structural analysis, bearing properties, vertical and horizontal load paths, displacement capacity, foundations, isolation gaps, architectural interfaces, and MEP utilities. The design must account for how the complete building behaves as a system.

For U.S. commercial, healthcare, industrial, and critical facilities, seismic isolation engineering also has to fit within the applicable building-code framework and project-specific criteria. ASCE 7, the International Building Code (IBC), the California Building Code (CBC), HCAI requirements for applicable California healthcare facilities, manufacturer engineering data, and Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ) requirements may all influence the final design.

The same principles apply differently to new construction and seismic isolation retrofit projects. New buildings can incorporate bearings, structural geometry, isolation gaps, utility crossings, and maintenance access from the beginning. Existing buildings introduce foundation limitations, temporary support requirements, restricted access, existing utilities, and field conditions that can significantly change the engineering approach.

A technically sound seismic isolation strategy therefore begins with the structure's performance objectives and ends with coordinated details that can actually be fabricated, installed, inspected, and maintained.

What Is Seismic Isolation Engineering?

Seismic isolation engineering is the process of designing and integrating an isolation system into a structure so that earthquake-induced motion and forces are managed through a controlled interface. In a typical base-isolated building, isolation bearings or related devices are positioned between the foundation or substructure and the superstructure. The isolation interface is engineered to support gravity loads while allowing specified horizontal movement during seismic excitation.

Seismic Isolation vs. Conventional Seismic Design

Conventional seismic design generally uses structural strength, stiffness, ductility, and energy dissipation to resist earthquake demands. Beams, columns, walls, frames, diaphragms, connections, and foundations are proportioned to develop the required load path and accommodate calculated forces and deformations.

Seismic isolation introduces another design variable: the behavior of the isolation interface. By modifying effective stiffness, period, damping, and force transmission, an isolation system can alter the seismic response experienced by the superstructure. This does not eliminate earthquake demand, but it changes how the demand is distributed between isolation movement, structural response, and other components.

How Seismic Base Isolation Works

A simplified dynamic model can represent an isolated structure as a mass supported by an engineered spring-and-damper system. The isolation system has effective horizontal properties, while the superstructure and foundation have their own stiffness and mass characteristics.

During an earthquake, the isolation interface can undergo substantial movement relative to the supporting ground. The superstructure is therefore not required to follow ground motion in exactly the same way as a fixed-base structure. Depending on the selected technology, the system may use elastomeric deformation, sliding, rolling, friction, damping, or a combination of mechanisms.

When Seismic Isolation Is Considered

Seismic isolation may be evaluated for hospitals, emergency facilities, critical infrastructure, specialized laboratories, commercial buildings, industrial facilities, and other structures where earthquake performance objectives justify the additional engineering complexity. It can also be investigated during seismic retrofit programs.

Isolation is not automatically appropriate for every building. Building height, mass distribution, site conditions, structural configuration, available displacement space, foundation conditions, architectural constraints, utility requirements, and project objectives all influence feasibility.

How Seismic Isolation Systems Change Structural Response

The value of seismic isolation comes from changing structural dynamics rather than simply adding a stronger structural component. Engineers therefore need to evaluate the entire isolated system, including the foundation, isolation bearings, superstructure, and components that cross the isolation interface.

Natural Period and Structural Response

One important concept is the effective natural period of the isolated structure. Introducing horizontal flexibility at the isolation level can increase the fundamental period compared with a conventional fixed-base configuration. The resulting change in dynamic characteristics can affect how the structure responds to earthquake ground motion.

The actual response depends on the isolation-system properties and the characteristics of the seismic input. Engineers must evaluate the effective stiffness, damping, mass, structural period, and applicable response criteria rather than assuming that a longer period automatically produces a particular performance outcome.

Base Shear and Force Transmission

Isolation can change the forces transmitted into the superstructure. However, the resulting force level is controlled by the complete dynamic system and the applicable analysis methodology. Bearing properties, damping assumptions, displacement, structural characteristics, and seismic design criteria all influence calculated response.

Displacement and Drift

An isolated building generally has to accommodate significant movement at the isolation interface. This creates a different design challenge from conventional interstory drift.

The isolation level requires sufficient clearance so that the superstructure can move without striking adjacent foundations, walls, stairs, architectural elements, utilities, or other obstructions. The required moat or isolation gap must therefore be coordinated across structural, architectural, and MEP drawings.

Damping and Energy Dissipation

Damping affects how seismic energy is dissipated and how displacement develops. Elastomeric systems, high-damping rubber bearings, lead-rubber bearings, and sliding technologies can exhibit substantially different response characteristics.

Because damping assumptions directly affect analytical results, engineers should use appropriate project-specific properties and manufacturer data rather than relying on generic values.

Seismic Isolation Bearing Types and System Components

Seismic isolation systems can use several technologies, each with different mechanical characteristics and design considerations. Bearing selection should be based on the complete structural requirement rather than on vertical capacity alone.

Elastomeric Bearings

Laminated elastomeric bearings typically consist of alternating layers of elastomer and steel reinforcement. The steel layers provide vertical load support and help control bulging, while the elastomer provides horizontal flexibility.

Natural rubber, synthetic rubber, and specialized elastomeric compounds can be formulated for different performance requirements. High-damping rubber bearings can provide additional damping characteristics, while lead-rubber configurations incorporate a lead core to contribute energy dissipation and hysteretic behavior.

Sliding Bearings

Sliding isolation systems accommodate seismic movement through controlled relative motion between surfaces. Low-friction materials such as PTFE or other engineered sliding interfaces may be used depending on system design.

Sliding systems require careful consideration of friction characteristics, vertical load, displacement, surface compatibility, temperature, wear, environmental exposure, and restoring behavior. Friction characteristics must be established for the actual design condition rather than assumed from a generic material description.

Roller and Ball Bearings

Rolling-based systems use rollers or balls to accommodate relative movement. Their engineering evaluation includes vertical capacity, alignment, movement range, rolling behavior, contact stresses, stability, and system configuration.

Seismic Expansion Joints

Bearings cannot function independently of the surrounding building. Seismic expansion joints, movement joints, flexible utility interfaces, and architectural separations may be required wherever relative movement occurs.

A technically adequate bearing design can still be compromised if an adjacent stair, pipe, duct, conduit, wall, or architectural element unintentionally restrains the isolation interface. System-level detailing is therefore essential.

Seismic Isolation System Design and Engineering Analysis

Seismic isolation system design begins with the building's seismic design criteria and develops toward project-specific bearing properties, structural reactions, displacement demands, and detailing requirements.

Seismic Hazard and Design Criteria

The engineering team establishes the applicable seismic parameters, site conditions, structural characteristics, building importance, and project performance objectives. Depending on the project, analysis may incorporate code-defined seismic parameters, site-specific hazard information, or additional project criteria.

Vertical Loads and Bearing Capacity

Each bearing must support its assigned gravity load while maintaining appropriate stability under the governing combinations. Engineers evaluate dead load, applicable live load, equipment loads, load distribution, eccentricity, and support-point reactions.

Vertical capacity alone does not determine suitability. A bearing that supports the required compression load may still be unsuitable if its horizontal stiffness, displacement capacity, damping, geometry, or environmental performance does not satisfy the system requirements.

Horizontal Stiffness and Effective Properties

Isolation calculations rely on appropriate effective properties. These can include horizontal stiffness, effective damping, friction characteristics, restoring behavior, and displacement-dependent response.

For elastomeric bearings, stiffness can be influenced by geometry, elastomer characteristics, temperature, loading, and deformation. Sliding systems require corresponding evaluation of friction and movement characteristics.

Maximum Displacement Demand

The isolation system must accommodate the calculated seismic displacement with adequate physical clearance. Engineers must coordinate bearing movement with moat gaps, structural supports, architectural elements, utility connections, stairs, elevators, and other interfaces.

Structural Analysis

Depending on the structure and applicable criteria, analysis may involve response-spectrum methods, equivalent static procedures where applicable, nonlinear static analysis, nonlinear response-history analysis, or other project-specific methods. The appropriate methodology depends on building characteristics, isolation-system behavior, code provisions, and performance objectives.

The output is not merely a bearing schedule. It must establish a coherent load path from the superstructure through the isolation system and into the foundation.

Isolation Interfaces, Foundations, and Structural Load Paths

An isolation system creates a structural interface that must remain functional under gravity loads, seismic forces, construction tolerances, and long-term service conditions.

Bearing Plates and Anchorage

Upper and lower bearing interfaces typically require carefully detailed plates, anchorages, structural attachments, and force-transfer components. Bearing plates may need custom dimensions and thicknesses based on calculated reactions and connection requirements.

Anchorage must transfer applicable forces without introducing unintended behavior into the isolation system. Connection design should therefore be integrated with bearing properties and structural analysis.

Foundations and Substructure

The supporting foundation must accommodate vertical reactions, lateral forces, construction tolerances, and the geometry of the isolation interface. Foundation stiffness and load distribution can affect the behavior of the system.

For retrofit projects, existing foundation capacity and configuration become especially important. Field verification may be required before finalizing reinforcement, bearing locations, or temporary support procedures.

Isolation Gaps and Moat Spaces

Isolation gaps must accommodate calculated movement without unintended contact. This requires more than simply drawing a gap around the building perimeter.

Stairs, ramps, facade elements, sidewalks, utility crossings, waterproofing, fire protection, and architectural finishes may all interact with the isolation interface. Each must be detailed to preserve the intended movement capacity.

Structural Continuity

Columns, walls, diaphragms, frames, and other structural elements above and below the isolation level must transfer gravity and seismic demands appropriately. Discontinuities introduced by the isolation plane require deliberate engineering rather than field improvisation.

Seismic Isolation for Buildings and Critical Facilities

Seismic isolation engineering can be evaluated across many building types, but the design objectives and coordination requirements vary by occupancy.

Commercial and Institutional Buildings

Office buildings, government facilities, educational buildings, and institutional structures may consider isolation when the project has specific seismic performance objectives or when architectural and structural requirements make the technology appropriate.

The design team must evaluate the building mass, structural geometry, foundation arrangement, isolation displacement, and utility interfaces early enough to avoid costly redesign.

Hospitals and Healthcare Facilities

Healthcare projects create particularly demanding coordination requirements because structural performance is only one part of post-earthquake functionality. Medical equipment, emergency systems, HVAC, electrical distribution, plumbing, fire protection, and communication systems may all need to remain operational.

California healthcare facilities may also fall under HCAI requirements and project-specific review processes. The former OSHPD terminology remains common in industry discussions, while HCAI is the current agency name. Isolation design should be coordinated with applicable healthcare structural, nonstructural, and facility requirements rather than treated as an isolated bearing-selection exercise.

Laboratories and Sensitive Facilities

Laboratories and precision environments may have stringent movement or vibration requirements. Isolation engineering in these facilities may need to be coordinated with sensitive instrumentation, mechanical systems, equipment foundations, and operational constraints.

Industrial and Manufacturing Facilities

Manufacturing plants, aerospace facilities, industrial buildings, and equipment-intensive facilities can present complex interactions between structural response and machinery performance. Building-level seismic isolation does not replace equipment-level seismic restraint or vibration isolation; each system must be evaluated according to its function.

Seismic Isolation Engineering for New Construction vs. Retrofit

The timing of isolation decisions has a major influence on engineering complexity. New construction generally provides more opportunities to integrate isolation into the structural concept, while retrofit projects must work within existing conditions.

New Construction

For a new building, bearing locations can be established as part of the initial structural layout. Foundations, columns, walls, diaphragms, isolation gaps, MEP crossings, architectural interfaces, and maintenance access can be coordinated before construction.

Early BIM and 3D CAD coordination can help identify conflicts between structural movement and utility routing. This is particularly valuable around the isolation plane, where a seemingly minor rigid connection can restrict intended movement.

Seismic Isolation Retrofit

Retrofit work may require structural jacking, temporary support, localized strengthening, foundation modifications, utility relocation, and staged construction. Existing drawings may not accurately represent current field conditions, making investigation and verification essential.

Existing utilities can be especially challenging because piping, electrical systems, fire protection, elevators, stairs, and architectural elements may cross the proposed isolation interface.

Retrofit Feasibility

A retrofit should begin with feasibility analysis rather than a presumption that isolation is practical. Engineers need to consider existing foundations, building configuration, structural condition, available access, occupancy, construction sequencing, utility constraints, required movement, and project objectives.

The engineering question is not simply whether bearings can physically be installed. It is whether the complete building can be modified to behave as a coordinated isolated structure.

MEP and Utility Coordination Across Isolation Interfaces

One of the most important aspects of seismic isolation engineering occurs outside the bearings themselves. Utilities crossing an isolation interface must accommodate relative movement without becoming unintended structural restraints.

Flexible Piping Connections

Piping systems may require flexible connections, movement-capable assemblies, appropriate supports, and coordinated anchorage. Pressure requirements, pipe diameter, operating conditions, movement direction, alignment, and seismic demands all affect the design.

A flexible component is only effective when the surrounding supports and connections permit the intended movement.

HVAC and Ductwork

Duct systems crossing an isolation plane require movement accommodation that is compatible with the expected seismic displacement. Rigid connections can transfer forces back into the isolated structure or restrict relative movement.

HVAC equipment also has separate seismic and vibration requirements. Pumps, fans, chillers, air handling units, and other mechanical equipment may require both vibration isolation and seismic restraint depending on their application.

Electrical and Communications Systems

Conduit, cable tray, electrical feeders, controls, communications systems, and associated supports must be coordinated where they cross the isolation interface. Movement-capable connections may be required to avoid damage or unintended restraint.

Plumbing and Fire Protection

Plumbing and fire protection systems require particular attention because continuity must be maintained while allowing relative movement. Pipe supports, flexible assemblies, fire-rated penetrations, and movement joints must be coordinated with the structural isolation geometry.

This is one reason seismic isolation projects benefit from multidisciplinary BIM coordination before fabrication and installation.

Seismic Isolation and U.S. Codes and Compliance Requirements

Seismic isolation engineering in the United States operates within a broader structural and regulatory framework. No single document should automatically be treated as a universal specification for every isolation system.

ASCE 7

ASCE 7 provides important structural loading and seismic design provisions used in U.S. building design. Its requirements must be applied according to the applicable edition, project conditions, and jurisdiction.

The standard also includes provisions affecting nonstructural components and equipment. These requirements are relevant when coordinating an isolated building with mechanical, electrical, and architectural systems.

IBC and CBC

The International Building Code provides the broader model-code framework used by many jurisdictions, while the California Building Code establishes requirements applicable to California projects based on the adopted code edition.

Engineers should always confirm the code edition and amendments adopted by the applicable jurisdiction rather than relying on a generic code reference.

HCAI / OSHPD Healthcare Requirements

California healthcare projects subject to HCAI requirements can involve additional review, documentation, and structural coordination requirements. Isolation systems, foundations, nonstructural components, MEP systems, and architectural interfaces may all require coordinated review.

AHJ and Project-Specific Criteria

Owner requirements, specifications, AHJ interpretations, manufacturer data, seismic qualification requirements, and project-specific structural criteria can influence the final system.

For this reason, engineering calculations and manufacturer documentation should be developed and reviewed as an integrated package.

How to Select a Seismic Isolation System

Selecting an isolation system requires evaluating the complete structural and operational environment rather than matching a bearing to a single load value.

Define Structural Requirements

The design team should establish building mass, structural configuration, seismic hazard, foundation conditions, bearing locations, expected reactions, displacement objectives, and applicable performance criteria.

Evaluate Bearing Properties

Important properties can include vertical capacity, horizontal stiffness, effective stiffness, damping, displacement capacity, restoring characteristics, stability, environmental limits, and durability.

Elastomeric and sliding systems can behave differently under temperature, loading, cycling, and long-term environmental exposure. Material compatibility and corrosion protection can therefore be important in industrial, marine, or chemically exposed environments.

Consider Installation and Maintenance

Bearing access, inspection, replacement, tolerances, installation sequence, and future maintenance should be addressed during design. A system that cannot be inspected or replaced within the project's physical constraints may create long-term maintenance problems.

Coordinate Manufacturer Data and Engineering Calculations

Catalog information is useful for preliminary evaluation, but project-specific calculations require actual structural reactions, movement demands, support conditions, and system properties. Manufacturer engineering data should be reviewed alongside structural calculations.

Evaluate Total System Performance

The final selection should consider bearings, bearing plates, foundations, structural framing, isolation gaps, architectural movement joints, flexible MEP connections, seismic restraints, and construction tolerances as one coordinated system.

BIM, Structural Engineering, and Custom Fabrication for Isolation Projects

Seismic isolation projects often require close coordination between engineering analysis and physical fabrication. The geometry of the isolation interface can influence bearing plates, support steel, anchors, MEP routing, access, and installation sequencing.

Structural Engineering and Seismic Calculations

Structural engineering establishes load paths, bearing reactions, structural response, displacement requirements, anchorage, foundation interfaces, and applicable seismic calculations. Wind and seismic design also need to remain coordinated where isolation components interact with the overall structural system.

BIM 3D CAD Coordination

BIM and 3D CAD modeling can represent bearings, structural framing, isolation gaps, utility crossings, architectural interfaces, support assemblies, and maintenance clearances. This provides a practical method for identifying clashes before fabrication or field installation.

Custom Bearing and Support Components

Project-specific conditions may require custom bearing plates, structural steel assemblies, brackets, equipment supports, anchor plates, or other fabricated components. Dimensions and connection details should follow the engineering design rather than being improvised in the field.

Metal Fabrication and Finishing

Custom components can involve stainless steel, carbon steel, aluminum, structural steel, high-strength steel, or alloy steel depending on the application. Fabrication processes may include laser cutting, plasma cutting, welding, forming, stamping, machining, galvanizing, and powder coating.

Common Seismic Isolation Engineering Challenges

Even a properly selected isolation technology can underperform if the surrounding design is incomplete or poorly coordinated.

Insufficient Displacement Clearance

One of the most common coordination risks is inadequate clearance. Architectural walls, stairs, facade elements, utility lines, sidewalks, and structural components must accommodate calculated isolation movement without creating unintended impact points.

Incorrect Bearing Selection

Selecting bearings solely according to vertical load capacity can overlook horizontal stiffness, damping, displacement, stability, environmental conditions, and load distribution. These parameters need to be evaluated together.

Incomplete Load Path Coordination

Forces must transfer continuously from the superstructure through bearing interfaces and into the foundation. Bearing plates, anchors, support steel, and concrete interfaces must therefore be designed as part of the load path.

Rigid Utility Connections

A rigid pipe, conduit, duct, or other utility crossing can unintentionally restrain the isolation system. This can alter the intended structural behavior and potentially introduce forces that were not included in the original design model.

Poor Construction Coordination

Differences between structural drawings, architectural details, MEP layouts, fabrication drawings, and field conditions can create serious conflicts around the isolation plane. Early coordination and field verification are particularly important.

Inadequate Retrofit Investigation

Existing-building isolation requires accurate information about foundations, framing, utilities, dimensions, material conditions, and access. Where drawings are incomplete, field investigation can be an essential part of engineering before final detailing.

Frequently Asked Questions About Seismic Isolation Engineering

What is seismic isolation engineering?

Seismic isolation engineering is the structural engineering process of designing and integrating an isolation interface that changes how earthquake motion is transmitted between the supporting ground or foundation and the superstructure. The work can include seismic analysis, bearing selection, displacement calculations, damping evaluation, structural load-path design, foundation coordination, anchorage, isolation-gap detailing, and MEP movement accommodation.

How does seismic base isolation work?

Seismic base isolation introduces controlled flexibility, sliding, rolling, damping, or related behavior between the foundation and superstructure. During an earthquake, the isolation system permits relative movement while supporting gravity loads and managing seismic response. By changing the effective dynamic characteristics of the structure, the system can alter forces and deformation experienced by the superstructure. It does not eliminate earthquake demand, and the isolation interface still has to be designed for calculated displacement, stability, force transfer, and clearance requirements.

What types of seismic isolation bearings are available?

Common technologies include laminated elastomeric bearings, high-damping rubber bearings, lead-rubber bearings, sliding bearings, friction-based systems, roller bearings, and ball-bearing configurations. Each has different stiffness, damping, displacement, restoring, friction, load-capacity, and environmental characteristics. Selection depends on the building's structural demands and the required behavior of the complete isolation system.

What is the difference between elastomeric and sliding seismic bearings?

Elastomeric bearings generally accommodate horizontal movement through deformation of an elastomeric material, often reinforced with steel plates. Sliding systems accommodate movement through relative motion between engineered surfaces. Their stiffness, damping, friction, restoring behavior, displacement characteristics, maintenance considerations, and environmental requirements can differ substantially. The appropriate technology depends on project-specific structural and performance criteria rather than a universal preference.

Is seismic isolation suitable for existing buildings?

Seismic isolation can be considered as a retrofit strategy, but feasibility depends heavily on the existing building. Engineers may need to evaluate foundations, structural framing, existing utilities, building geometry, access, occupancy, temporary support, construction sequencing, and required movement. Retrofit isolation can require substantial modification, so a feasibility and existing-condition investigation should precede detailed system selection.

What standards apply to seismic isolation engineering in the United States?

Applicable requirements can include ASCE 7, the IBC, state and local building codes, the CBC for California projects, HCAI requirements for applicable healthcare facilities, project specifications, owner criteria, AHJ requirements, and manufacturer engineering documentation. The specific requirements depend on the jurisdiction, adopted code edition, occupancy, project type, and structural design criteria.

Does seismic isolation eliminate seismic forces?

No. Seismic isolation does not eliminate earthquake forces or structural demand. Instead, it modifies the dynamic response of the structure and changes how motion and forces are accommodated. The isolated building still requires structural design, foundation design, bearing capacity, displacement evaluation, anchorage, and coordination of nonstructural components and utilities.

How much displacement does a seismic isolation system need to accommodate?

The required displacement is project-specific. It depends on seismic hazard, building mass, structural characteristics, isolation-system stiffness, damping or friction behavior, analysis methodology, and applicable design criteria. The calculated displacement must then be translated into physical clearance requirements at the isolation interface. Architectural elements, structural components, stairs, elevators, utilities, and MEP systems must all accommodate the expected movement.

How are MEP systems coordinated with seismic isolation?

MEP systems crossing an isolation interface may require flexible piping connections, movement-capable ductwork, flexible electrical connections, coordinated fire-protection piping, seismic expansion joints, and other movement-accommodating details. Supports must also be reviewed so they do not unintentionally restrain the building. The objective is to maintain utility function while allowing the relative movement anticipated by the structural design.

Can seismic isolation be combined with seismic bracing?

Yes. Building-level seismic isolation and nonstructural seismic bracing serve different purposes. An isolated building can still contain HVAC equipment, piping, ductwork, conduit, cable tray, and other components that require seismic restraint or bracing. MEP trapeze systems, HVAC duct bracing, pipe supports, conduit bracing, cable tray bracing, and strut channel systems should be coordinated with the movement and structural behavior of the isolated building.

What information is needed for seismic isolation system design?

Typical design inputs include building mass, structural configuration, seismic design criteria, site conditions, bearing locations, vertical reactions, structural properties, displacement requirements, foundation conditions, isolation-gap geometry, MEP crossings, architectural constraints, environmental exposure, and applicable code requirements. Manufacturer engineering data is also important once the required system properties have been established.

Conclusion

Seismic isolation engineering is a system-level structural discipline, not simply a process of choosing seismic bearings. The isolation interface influences structural dynamics, displacement, force transmission, foundation behavior, architectural separation, utility movement, and nonstructural seismic performance. A successful design therefore requires coordination from the initial seismic analysis through bearing selection, structural detailing, MEP integration, fabrication, installation, and long-term access.

For engineers evaluating seismic isolation systems, the most important starting point is the building's actual performance requirement. Site seismic conditions, structural mass, stiffness, vertical reactions, displacement demand, damping characteristics, foundation configuration, and available isolation space establish the technical framework for system selection. Elastomeric, high-damping rubber, lead-rubber, sliding, roller, and ball-bearing technologies can each serve different engineering objectives, but none should be selected independently of the complete load path.

The isolation interface also extends well beyond the structural drawings. Piping, ductwork, electrical systems, fire protection, stairs, elevators, architectural finishes, and other components must accommodate relative movement. In healthcare and critical facilities, these interfaces can be particularly important because post-earthquake functionality depends on more than the survival of the primary structural frame.

For new construction, early integration can simplify bearing placement, foundations, isolation gaps, BIM coordination, and utility routing. For retrofit projects, field investigation and feasibility analysis become equally important because existing foundations, utilities, access conditions, and occupancy can constrain the available solutions.
